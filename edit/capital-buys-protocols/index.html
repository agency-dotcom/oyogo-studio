Women are 84% of solo travellers. The industry is still charging them a single supplement.

Sunday 10 August 2026 The thesis ≈ 6 min read

This week the highest-spending guest in wellness hospitality told us, unprompted, that nobody understands the brief.

Meanwhile $3.8 billion of capital went somewhere else entirely.

We posted about the solo female wellness traveller this week. It became our most-engaged post of the month. But the comments were better than the post, and one of them has been bothering us since.

A reader, a decade into travelling solo:

Despite a lot more choice now, I still don't think many are understanding the brief.

And another, more bluntly:

As much as I want to get away, the prices are too expensive for being solo.

That is not a niche complaint from the edge of the market. It is the single highest-spending guest in wellness hospitality, telling an industry that is currently deploying billions of dollars that it is building for somebody else — and charging her extra to watch.

Who she actually is

The numbers are not marginal, and they are not new. They are simply not being designed around.

Women are 84% of all solo travellers globally. The average solo female traveller is 57 — squarely inside the highest-spending demographic in wellness tourism. Those aged 45 and over spend an average of $18,000 per trip. Searches for "solo female travel" rose 131% in a single year. Women are projected to control 75% of discretionary spending by 2028.

Fifty-four per cent of affluent travellers earning over $250,000 are women.

So: the biggest segment, the oldest and wealthiest cohort, the fastest-growing search term, and the person who will control three quarters of the money within two years. And the standard industry response is a room rate designed for two people with a surcharge for arriving alone.

What the industry is buying instead

Here is where the week's other news becomes relevant, because it explains the distraction.

On 4 August, Procter & Gamble agreed to pay US$3.8 billion in cash for Thorne, the science-led supplements brand — 5.6× what L Catterton paid for it in 2023. Thorne sells through practitioners, gyms and longevity clinics: recovery, sleep, healthy ageing, metabolic health. The supplement shelf behind the treatment room has just been repriced as strategic infrastructure by the largest consumer goods company in the world.

The same week, a 59,000 sq ft spa opened at the Park Hyatt Cabo del Sol, billed as the largest in the region. In Tuscany, Terme di Saturnia committed €15m and launched a Longevity Score built on genetic testing, oxidative stress measurement and sleep studies.

PwC's mid-year deals outlook caught the logic in a sentence. Hospitality M&A volume was down 2.5%, yet upscale-and-above assets took 73% of transactions, the highest concentration in two years. Their Jonathan Shing:

Five years ago, the physical asset was the deal. Today, the asset is half the deal. The other half is the data.

Capital has worked out that the measurable part is the valuable part. That is a genuinely good instinct. It is also entirely orthogonal to what the woman in the comments was asking for.

The spending is not producing the returns anyway

Two datasets landed in the same fortnight, and both suggest the building spree is misjudged.

ISPA's 2026 US Spa Industry Study recorded a record US$23.5bn in 2025 revenue — up 4.2%, but down from the prior year's 5.8%. Underneath it: 191 million visits, against 192 million in 2019. Average spend up 2.3% to $123. Locations up 0.4%.

Demand has flatlined at pre-pandemic volume. The growth is price, not people.

RLA Global's 2025 report, built on property-level data across more than 13,000 hotels, is sharper still. Hotels with a smaller wellness offer out-earned the destination-spa properties on every line simultaneously — $250 ADR against $232, $170 RevPAR against $161, $99 GOPPAR against $89. The first year the lighter-touch model has won on rate, revenue and profit at once.

Bigger is not working. And nobody spending $59,000 sq ft worth of capital appears to have asked the guest with $18,000 in her pocket what she actually wants.

What she said she wants

Read the thread again and it is not complicated.

She isn't retreating from the world when she travels alone — she is moving toward something. Community. Connection. The particular belonging that comes from being in the right room with the right people. Another commenter, a retreat host: "shaping new connections and friendships with like-minded souls."

That is a programming problem and a pricing problem. Neither is solved by square footage, and neither shows up in a diagnostics suite.

The most valuable guest in wellness travel is asking for a table she can sit at alone without paying double. The industry is answering with a bigger spa and a blood panel.

The honest read

Drop the single supplement, or price the room per person. It is the clearest, cheapest signal available that you have understood who is actually booking. Every operator reading this could change it on Monday.

Programme for one. A communal table with a host. A morning group run or walk. Classes that are genuinely social rather than parallel. The thing she is buying is a room with the right people in it, and that is an operating decision, not a capital one.

Measurement still matters — but it is not the whole answer. The protocol shift is real and we would back it. Just notice that the guest with the most money is describing a different problem, and it is a considerably cheaper one to fix.

Best for: operators with a capital budget and an assumption that a bigger spa is the answer.

Less suited to: anyone hoping the fix is another diagnostic. This one is programming and pricing.
