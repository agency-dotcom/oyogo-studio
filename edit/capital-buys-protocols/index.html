Sunday insight

Capital has stopped buying buildings. It has started buying protocols.

Sunday 10 August 2026 The thesis ≈ 6 min read

Procter & Gamble paid $3.8 billion for a supplements maker in the same week a 59,000 sq ft spa opened in Baja.

Only one of those tells you where the money in wellness is actually going.

On 4 August, Procter & Gamble agreed to pay US$3.8 billion in cash for Thorne, a science-led supplements brand. On the same day, a 59,000 sq ft spa opened at the Park Hyatt Cabo del Sol — billed as the largest in the region. Two wellness stories, one news cycle. Only one of them is a signal.

The spa is the thing that photographs. The supplements company is the thing that got repriced.

What P&G actually bought

Thorne sells through practitioners, gyms and longevity clinics as much as through retail. L Catterton paid US$680m for it in October 2023; P&G is paying 5.6× that, on revenue growing from roughly US$290m to an expected US$650m this year.

The 250-product range spans recovery, sleep, healthy ageing and metabolic health — precisely the categories resort spas and longevity clinics now retail and prescribe.

So the supplement shelf behind the treatment room has just been valued at nearly four billion dollars by the largest consumer goods company in the world. That shelf used to be an afterthought in the spa P&L. It is now strategic infrastructure, and margin expectations along that channel will follow.

Meanwhile, the spa itself is getting bigger and earning less

Two datasets landed in the same fortnight, and they point the same way.

ISPA's 2026 US Spa Industry Study put 2025 revenue at a record US$23.5bn — up 4.2%, but down from the prior year's 5.8%. The detail underneath is the story: 191 million visits, against 192 million in 2019. Average spend rose 2.3% to $123. Locations grew 0.4%.

Demand has normalised at pre-pandemic volume. The growth is coming from price, not footfall.

RLA Global's 2025 Wellness Real Estate Report, built on property-level HotStats data across more than 13,000 hotels, is blunter still. Hotels with a smaller wellness offer out-earned the destination-spa properties on every line at once:

$250 ADR against $232. $170 RevPAR against $161. $99 GOPPAR against $89.

First year the lighter-touch format has beaten the full destination-spa model on rate, revenue and profit simultaneously. Capital intensity is outrunning rate.

Put those beside the P&G number and a pattern appears. Money is leaving the square footage and moving toward the measurable, repeatable, take-home parts.

What's being bought instead: the number

In Tuscany, Terme di Saturnia is spending €15m across 2026–27 on the 124-key thermal resort. Four thermal-pool junior suites, a Roman bath with Aufguss rituals, a Studio Marco Piva welcome area.

But the substance isn't the marble. It's Saturnia Natural Longevity — genetic testing, skin analysis, oxidative stress measurement, sleep studies and body composition, resolved into a single Longevity Score that is re-measured over time. The operator is targeting revenue above €35m by end-2027, from €28.2m in 2025.

A score is a very different product from a treatment. A treatment ends when you leave. A score follows you home, and gives you a reason to come back and move it.

PwC put the same shift in one line, in its mid-year US deals outlook. Hospitality M&A volume was down 2.5%, yet upscale-and-above assets took 73% of transactions — the highest concentration in two years. Their Jonathan Shing:

Five years ago, the physical asset was the deal. Today, the asset is half the deal. The other half is the data.

And the guest who is buying it

We posted this week about the solo female wellness traveller. It became our most-engaged post of the month — over 1,270 likes, and a comment thread that was more useful than the post.

The numbers are stark enough on their own: women are 84% of all solo travellers globally, the average solo female traveller is 57, and those aged 45 and over spend an average of $18,000 per trip. Searches for "solo female travel" rose 131% in a year. Women are projected to control 75% of discretionary spending by 2028.

But it was the replies that mattered. One reader, a decade into travelling solo: "despite a lot more choice now, I still don't think many are understanding the brief." Another, plainly: "as much as I want to get away, the prices are too expensive for being solo."

That is the highest-value guest in wellness hospitality telling you, unprompted, that the category is being built for someone else — and pricing her out with single supplements while it does it.

She is also exactly the buyer for a protocol. She isn't paying $18,000 for square footage. She is paying for something to be different afterwards.

Capital has worked out that the measurable part is the valuable part. Most operators are still building the part that photographs.

The honest read

Three things follow, and none of them are comfortable if you have just signed off a large spa.

Square footage is no longer the flex. Aman's new Amanvari in Baja runs a 660 sq m spa across just 18 casitas. The ratio is the strategy. Meanwhile the region's largest spa opened the same week at a 163-key hotel. One of those is a yield decision; the other is a headline.

If nothing is measured, nothing is being sold but atmosphere. Atmosphere is a real product, and there is nothing wrong with selling it — but it should be priced as atmosphere, not as outcome.

The guest is ahead of the industry. She already has a wearable, probably a blood panel, and an opinion about her own sleep. She arrives knowing her numbers. The question every operator now faces is what you can tell her that she doesn't already know.

Best for: operators deciding what to do with a capital budget, and anyone weighing a wellness stay on what it will actually change.

Less suited to: the assumption that a bigger spa is a better business. This fortnight's data says the opposite.
